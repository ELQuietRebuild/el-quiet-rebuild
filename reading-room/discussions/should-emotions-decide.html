DISCUSSION 01 · EMOTIONAL INTELLIGENCE

Should Your Emotions Decide What You Do Next?

A thoughtful discussion about emotion, responsibility and the person you are becoming.

There are moments when an emotion seems to fill the entire situation.

You are angry. You are hurt. You are embarrassed.

Someone has said something that feels unfair. Someone has disappointed you. Perhaps you have been criticized in a way that felt disrespectful. Or maybe you made a mistake and someone confronted you about it.

In that moment, what you feel can seem like the most important thing in the room.

And perhaps it deserves to be felt.

But does it deserve to decide what happens next?

That is a different question.

Feeling Something Is Not the Same as Giving It Control

We sometimes speak about emotional strength as though strong people simply stop feeling certain things.

Don't be hurt. Don't be embarrassed. Don't get angry. Don't care what people think .

But being human does not work so neatly.

Something can hurt you even when you wish it didn't. You can understand that a situation will eventually pass and still feel embarrassed by it. You can know who you are and still be affected by another person's opinion.

Perhaps the goal is not to become someone who feels nothing.

Maybe part of emotional maturity is being able to say:

This hurt me.

And then ask:

What am I going to do with that feeling?

Because experiencing an emotion and giving that emotion authority over your next decision are not the same thing.

You can feel something for a moment without allowing it to take over so completely that you forget who you are—or who you are becoming.

The Moment Between Feeling and Responding

Imagine that you are extremely angry with someone.

You could respond immediately.

You could say everything that comes into your mind while the anger is fresh. Perhaps part of you even wants the other person to feel some of the hurt you are experiencing.

But what happens after that moment?

Eventually, you may calm down.

The words you said do not disappear with the anger.

You may have said something you regret. You may have damaged a relationship, embarrassed someone, sent a message you cannot take back, or created consequences that are much more difficult to undo than the original disagreement.

The emotion may have lasted twenty minutes.

The consequence may last much longer.

That does not mean the emotion was wrong.

It means the emotion may not have been the best thing to put in charge of the decision.

Say nothing for the moment. Leave. Calm down. Think. Then decide.

From the outside, that may look like doing nothing. But choosing not to react when every part of you wants to react can require more discipline than responding immediately.

Calming Down Is Not the End

We could leave every difficult situation, calm ourselves down, and return convinced that the other person was the problem.

That isn't necessarily emotional intelligence either.

Creating distance gives us an opportunity to examine what happened after the intensity has reduced.

And then some uncomfortable questions become possible.

Why did that affect me so strongly?

Was there any truth in what was said?

Sometimes the answer will be no.

But sometimes it will be yes.

Perhaps someone pointed out something you genuinely need to work on. Perhaps you were wrong. Perhaps your behaviour hurt someone. Perhaps an apology is necessary.

Being hurt by the way criticism was delivered does not automatically make the criticism false.

If there is something we need to change, emotional maturity should help us recognize it—not protect us from having to face it.

When Two Things Are True

We often want a clear answer: who was right and who was wrong?

But sometimes two things are true at once.

You were wrong.

And the way the other person treated you was also wrong.

One truth does not erase the other.

If I have done something wrong, I can acknowledge it. I can apologize. I can make things right where possible. I can work on myself.

But taking responsibility for my behaviour does not require me to pretend that someone else's disrespectful behaviour was acceptable.

And someone else's poor behaviour does not give me permission to avoid responsibility for mine.

Instead of asking only:

Who was wrong?

Perhaps we should also ask:

What was I responsible for?

What were they responsible for?

That allows us to take responsibility without automatically taking responsibility for everything.

Sometimes the Wisest Decision Is to Leave

Not every disagreement needs to be solved in the moment.

And not every disagreement is safe.

If a situation appears to be moving toward aggression or violence, proving that you are right is no longer the priority.

You can leave. You can go somewhere safe. You can think about what happened later.

Leaving does not always mean weakness, just as staying does not always mean strength.

Sometimes good judgment means recognizing what the situation requires.

Bouncing Back Doesn't Mean Nothing Happened

There is something appealing about the idea of bouncing back.

Something hurts us, we feel it for a while, and eventually we return to ourselves.

But bouncing back does not have to mean pretending the experience never happened.

Maybe criticism exposed something you need to work on. Maybe a difficult conversation showed you that you need stronger boundaries. Maybe your own reaction revealed an insecurity you had not understood. Maybe you owe someone an apology.

Or perhaps the situation showed you something you no longer want to tolerate.

You can recover from an experience without allowing it to control you.

And you can recover without refusing to learn from it.

The Person You Are Becoming

This brings us to the question underneath the entire discussion.

When you are hurt, angry, embarrassed, disappointed or afraid, who should make the final decision?

The emotion you are experiencing right now?

Or the person you are becoming?

My answer is: the person I am becoming.

Because the future matters more than the present situation.

That does not mean the present is unimportant.

It means a temporary emotional state should not automatically be allowed to create consequences that conflict with the life, character, standards and relationships you are trying to build.

The person you are becoming may still decide to speak. She may apologize. She may confront disrespect. She may establish a boundary. She may leave. She may forgive. She may admit that she needs to change.

Or she may decide that something simply isn't worth carrying any further.

Emotional maturity does not always produce the same response.

What changes is who—or what—is making the decision.

There is now space between what I feel and what I choose.

And perhaps much of emotional maturity is built inside that space.

SIT WITH THIS

Before You Leave This Discussion

Think about a recent moment when you were hurt, angry, embarrassed, disappointed or deeply frustrated.

What did the emotion want you to do immediately?

What did you actually do?

What happened because of that decision?

Once you were calmer, was there any truth you needed to acknowledge?

Did someone else's behaviour stop you from examining your own?

If the person you are becoming had made the decision, would she have chosen differently?

You don't have to answer immediately.

Some questions are worth sitting with.
