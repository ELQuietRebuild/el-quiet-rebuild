Critical Thinking & Knowledge

As a woman, you may be making decisions about your money, relationships, career, family, personal growth and future while receiving different messages about what you should do, believe or become. Some of those messages may come from family, culture, social media, people you respect, or even beliefs you have carried for years.

I hear different opinions, advice and information, but I'm not always sure what to believe or how to form my own position.

What can make clear thinking difficult?

We live around information every day. We hear opinions from family and friends, watch videos online, read posts, listen to experts, follow traditions and receive advice about money, relationships, careers, health and life.

But hearing something repeatedly does not automatically make it true.

Critical thinking is not about distrusting everything or trying to prove everyone wrong. It is about learning to slow down, ask better questions, examine the information available and think before reaching a conclusion.

It also means being willing to examine your own thinking, not only the thinking of other people.

Sometimes we accept an idea because it comes from someone we respect, because many people around us believe it, or because it agrees with something we already believe. At other times, we may reject information too quickly because it challenges our existing views.

Our experiences, emotions, upbringing, environment and assumptions can influence how we interpret information. This does not mean we should ignore our experiences. It means we should learn to recognise when they may be influencing our conclusions.

Developing critical thinking helps us become more deliberate about what we accept, what we question and why.

What can you learn and practise?

  • Identify the actual claim being made.
  • Ask what evidence supports that claim.
  • Consider whether the source is knowledgeable and reliable.
  • Separate facts from opinions, interpretations and personal experiences.
  • Notice assumptions, including your own.
  • Ask what important information might be missing.
  • Consider other reasonable explanations or perspectives.
  • Change or refine your position when stronger information becomes available.
  • Explain not only what you think, but why you think it.

Changing your mind after receiving better information is not necessarily a weakness. Sometimes it is evidence that you are willing to learn.

A simple framework for examining an idea

When you encounter a claim, piece of advice or strong opinion, you can slow your thinking down by asking:

CLAIM → EVIDENCE → SOURCE → ASSUMPTIONS → OTHER PERSPECTIVES → CONCLUSION

Claim

What exactly is being said? Before deciding whether you agree, make sure you understand the actual claim.

Evidence

What supports it? Look for information, examples, research, records or other evidence that can be examined rather than relying only on confidence or repetition.

Source

Where did the information come from? Ask whether the source has relevant knowledge, whether the information can be checked, and whether other reliable sources support or challenge it.

Assumptions

What is being taken for granted? Sometimes an argument depends on something being assumed rather than demonstrated. Your own assumptions matter too.

Other perspectives

Is there another reasonable way to understand this? Considering another perspective does not mean you must agree with it. It helps you understand the issue more completely.

Conclusion

What does the available information actually justify? You do not always need to reach a strong conclusion immediately. Sometimes the most reasonable position is: "I don't have enough information yet."

Try this

Consider this statement:

"If many people believe something, it is probably true."

Instead of immediately agreeing or disagreeing, work through the framework.

Claim: What is this statement actually suggesting?

Evidence: What evidence would you need before accepting it?

Source: Does the reliability of the people or sources repeating the information matter?

Assumptions: What is the statement assuming about popular beliefs?

Other perspectives: Can you think of situations where many people believed something that later turned out to be incorrect, or situations where widespread agreement was supported by strong evidence?

Conclusion: After examining it, what position would you take, and what is your reasoning?

The goal is not to find the perfect answer. The goal is to practise how you arrive at an answer.
