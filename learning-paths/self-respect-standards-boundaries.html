Self-Respect, Standards & Boundaries

There are times when keeping the peace, being liked, avoiding conflict or trying not to disappoint others can make it difficult to speak up for yourself.

You may tolerate things that repeatedly make you uncomfortable, say yes when you want to say no, or lower a standard because you are afraid of losing a relationship, an opportunity or someone's approval.

But there is another side worth examining too. The standards you expect from other people should also make you think about the standards you practise yourself.

“I want to become clearer about what matters to me, what I will and will not accept, and the standards I want to practise in my own life.”

Understanding the difference

Self-respect concerns how you regard and treat yourself.

Standards are the expectations and principles you choose for your behaviour, relationships and life.

Boundaries help you decide where your limits are and what you will do when an important limit is crossed.

These ideas are connected, but they are not only about what other people should do.

For example, if respectful communication is one of your standards, you can expect it from others while also examining how respectfully you communicate during disagreement.

If you value honesty, responsibility or consideration, the question becomes not only:

“Do other people give me this?”

but also:

“How am I practising this value myself?”

Where did your standards come from?

Not everything we consider normal was consciously chosen.

Family, culture, relationships, past experiences, social environments and things we repeatedly hear can influence what we believe we should accept, reject or expect.

Some of those standards may still serve us well. Others may need examination.

Before deciding, “This is my standard,” it can be useful to ask:

Why does this matter to me?

What value is underneath it?

Is it reasonable?

Do I practise the principle I expect from others?

This is where self-respect becomes more than declaring what you will not tolerate. It becomes part of understanding how you want to live.

Boundaries are also about your actions

A boundary is not simply telling another person what they must do.

You cannot completely control another person's behaviour, but you can decide how you will participate in a situation.

For example:

“You are not allowed to speak to me like that.”

focuses mainly on controlling the other person's behaviour.

Whereas:

“If the conversation becomes disrespectful, I will end the conversation and we can continue when we can speak respectfully.”

also identifies what you will do.

A meaningful boundary therefore requires more than words. Your actions need to support what you say matters to you.

Not every uncomfortable situation requires a boundary

This is important too.

Feedback can be uncomfortable. Compromise can be uncomfortable. Disagreement can be uncomfortable. Hearing something we do not want to hear can be uncomfortable.

Sometimes discomfort signals that something genuinely needs to change. At other times, discomfort is simply part of learning, communicating and living alongside other people.

So instead of immediately asking:

“How do I protect myself from this?”

you can also ask:

“What is actually happening here, and what does this situation require from me?”

That may lead to a boundary, a conversation, a compromise, a change in your own behaviour—or simply greater understanding.

What can you learn and practise?

  • Recognise situations that repeatedly leave you uncomfortable or resentful.
  • Understand the values behind your standards.
  • Distinguish a preference from an important limit.
  • Say no without constructing a long defence.
  • Communicate expectations respectfully.
  • Recognise when compromise is reasonable.
  • Notice when fear of disapproval is influencing your choices.
  • Consider whether you practise the principles you expect from others.

Use the EL Quiet Rebuild framework

LEARN → THINK → REBUILD → APPLY → BECOME

LEARN — What is happening?

Look at the situation or pattern before immediately reacting to it.

THINK — What matters here, and why?

Examine the value, belief or standard involved.

REBUILD — What needs to change?

Perhaps a boundary needs strengthening. Perhaps an expectation needs adjusting. Perhaps something in your own behaviour needs attention too.

APPLY — What will I do?

Communicate, make a decision or take an action consistent with what you have identified.

BECOME — What does this require from me?

Consider the qualities and behaviours you need to develop if you want to live by the standards you say matter to you.

Try this

Choose one situation where you feel that a standard or boundary may be needed.

What is happening?
Describe the situation without immediately judging the whole person.

What matters to me here?
Identify the value or standard underneath your reaction.

Where did this standard come from?
Have I thoughtfully chosen it, or have I simply become accustomed to believing it?

What is within my control?
Think about your communication, participation and actions.

What does this require from me?
If I expect this principle from someone else, how am I practising it myself?

Then decide what reasonable action the situation requires.

Your standards are not only statements about what you expect from the world. They also help shape the person you are becoming.

Continue practising

Standards and boundaries become clearer when you practise thinking through real situations. Consider each scenario before deciding what the situation actually requires.

1. Someone gives you respectful feedback that makes you uncomfortable. Before calling it a boundary issue, what would you examine about the situation and your reaction?

WHAT IS HAPPENING? → WHAT MATTERS? → WHAT IS WITHIN MY CONTROL? → WHAT ACTION IS NEEDED?

The aim is not to create a rule for every uncomfortable situation. It is to become more thoughtful about what you accept, what you communicate, what you practise yourself and how you respond.
