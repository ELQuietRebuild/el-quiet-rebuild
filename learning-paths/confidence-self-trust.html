Confidence & Self-Trust

Many women find themselves carrying responsibilities, making important decisions and trying to build a better future while still questioning whether they can trust their own judgment. You might know what you want to do, yet find yourself reconsidering it repeatedly, asking others for reassurance, comparing your choices with theirs, or hesitating because you are afraid of making the wrong decision.

I want to trust myself more, make decisions with greater confidence, and stop constantly second-guessing myself.

What can make self-trust difficult?

Self-trust does not mean believing that every decision you make will be correct. It means developing confidence in your ability to think, decide, act, learn and respond to what happens next.

Sometimes self-doubt develops because we have made mistakes in the past, received criticism, become accustomed to depending on other people's opinions, or have not yet developed enough knowledge or experience in a particular area.

There is nothing wrong with asking for advice. Other people can offer knowledge, experience and perspectives that we do not have.

The difficulty begins when we become unable to make ordinary decisions without repeatedly looking for someone else to confirm that we are doing the right thing.

Self-trust grows when you begin gathering your own evidence: you make decisions, take action, observe the results, learn from what happens and adjust when necessary.

You gradually discover:

“I may not know everything, but I can learn, make thoughtful decisions and handle what comes next.”

What can you learn and practise?

  • Make smaller decisions without repeatedly asking others to confirm them.
  • Listen to advice without automatically handing your decision over to someone else.
  • Separate “I don't know enough yet” from “I don't trust myself.”
  • Build knowledge and skills in areas where lack of competence is creating uncertainty.
  • Keep small commitments you make to yourself.
  • Recognise evidence of your own progress and capability.
  • Accept that making a decision always involves some uncertainty.
  • Reflect on mistakes without using them as proof that you are incapable.
  • Change direction when new information shows that an adjustment is needed.
  • Ask for help when help is genuinely useful without becoming dependent on reassurance.

Confidence is not the same as certainty

You can feel uncertain and still make a thoughtful decision.

For example, imagine you are considering applying for a new opportunity. You may think:

“What if I'm not ready?”

Confidence does not necessarily answer:

“I'm definitely ready. Everything will work out.”

A more grounded response might be:

“I don't know exactly what will happen, but I can examine what the opportunity requires, identify what I already know, recognise what I still need to learn, and decide whether taking the next step makes sense.”

That is a different kind of confidence. It is based less on feeling fearless and more on developing the ability to think and respond.

Build confidence through evidence

Sometimes we wait to feel confident before doing something. But confidence can also develop in the opposite direction:

ACTION → EXPERIENCE → EVIDENCE → CONFIDENCE

You try something. You gain experience. You discover what you can do and what you still need to improve. That experience gives you evidence.

Over time, the evidence can change the way you see your own capability.

This is why learning a skill, completing something difficult, keeping a commitment, speaking even when nervous, or recovering after a mistake can contribute to self-trust.

A simple self-trust framework

DECIDE → ACT → OBSERVE → LEARN → ADJUST → TRUST

Decide

What decision actually needs to be made?

Separate the real decision from all the fears surrounding it. Ask what you know, what you still need to know and what matters most in this situation.

Act

What reasonable action can you take?

Not every decision requires a dramatic step. Sometimes you only need to take the next sensible action.

Observe

What happened?

Pay attention to the result instead of immediately labelling yourself a success or failure.

Learn

What did the experience teach you?

Look at what worked, what did not work and what you understand now that you did not understand before.

Adjust

What should you change?

Self-trust does not require stubbornly continuing with a poor decision. Sometimes trusting yourself means recognising new information and changing direction.

Trust

What evidence have you gained about yourself?

Remember the decisions you handled, problems you solved, skills you developed, difficult moments you came through and lessons you applied. Your evidence matters.

Try this

Think of one decision you are currently second-guessing.

Decision: What am I actually trying to decide?

What I know: What facts or information do I already have?

What I don't know: Is there genuinely important information missing?

Advice: Do I need useful information from someone else, or am I mainly looking for reassurance?

Next action: What is one reasonable step I can take?

Evidence: What previous experience shows that I am capable of thinking, learning or adjusting if things do not go exactly as planned?

Then make the best decision you can with the information available to you.

You do not have to prove that you will never make a mistake. The deeper practice is learning:

“I can make a thoughtful decision, take responsibility for it, learn from the result and adjust when necessary.”
